## The song first

“SONORA” is two and a half minutes long and doesn’t waste any of it.

The song is written as a direct address. The narrator is talking to another woman about the man they’ve both been seeing, and she lays it all out: the dinners all over LA, the shopping, the late nights, the weekend he said he was working. It isn’t a plea or a confession. It’s a heads-up, delivered by someone who has already blocked his number and moved on to the part where she tells you about it.

What makes it work is how plainly it’s written. The verses stack up concrete details instead of feelings, and they let the listener do the math. The hook carries her own name, “u can tell him cris told ya,” so the song is signed inside its own chorus. And the refrain “that’s none of my biz” does exactly the opposite of what it says, which is both the joke and the point. It takes confidence to write a song that short and that direct and trust it to land.

The cover frame sets the tone before anything plays: a black cap, black lace, a black vintage car, a dark studio. Nothing in it is softened, and neither is the song.

The video was directed by Frankie Fire, with Rai Angle and Ray WAllie producing and Parke Fox as director of photography. You can stream the song at [createmusic.fm/sonora](https://createmusic.fm/sonora).

## Cristiana and Overwatch

Cristiana is also the CEO of Overwatch Technologies.

We’re mentioning that here, after the music, on purpose. “SONORA” didn’t need us, and nothing in this article should suggest otherwise. What her role reflects is something we want Overwatch to pay closer attention to: the people whose work reaches an audience, and what it takes to keep that connection honest.

## Finding the real thing

A release like “SONORA” doesn’t live in one place. There’s the video on YouTube, a streaming link, a link page, and whatever gets shared, clipped, and reposted after that. Every one of those is a point where a listener can be pointed somewhere else.

For independent artists this is a familiar problem. Around a new release, lookalike accounts, unofficial re-uploads, and fake “promo” or giveaway links tend to show up, and to a casual listener they can look a lot like the real thing. The cost lands on the audience, who might hand over a login or a payment, and on the artist, whose name is attached to something she never made.

That’s the corner of cybersecurity we want to explore for artists and brands: making it easy for listeners to find authentic channels, and making it harder for someone to impersonate them. It starts with the basics, like strong, recoverable access to the accounts that hold the releases, and grows from there.

## What we’ve done, and what we’re exploring

To be clear about where we are: **Overwatch hasn’t assessed Cristiana’s accounts, channels, or systems, and nothing in this article describes such an assessment.**

Overwatch’s security work so far is foundational. It includes certification study, hands-on practice on training platforms like TryHackMe and LetsDefend, and lab work with devices we own, all on our own equipment or in isolated environments. You can read about that path in [From Software to Silicon](/from-software-to-silicon-cybersecurity-roadmap/).

Here’s what we intend to explore. None of it is finished:

- **A plain-language account checklist for artists:** multi-factor authentication, recovery options, and safe ways to share access with collaborators and teams.
- **Spotting impersonation using public information only:** recognizing lookalike channels and fraudulent promotional links, and knowing how to report them.
- **Making official channels easy to verify:** for example, one canonical link page that every official profile points back to.
- **Respectful artist websites:** sites that are secure by default and collect only the audience data they actually need.

## How we intend to work

Promotion should serve the artist, not overwrite her. That means her voice stays hers, her work and accounts stay under her ownership, and nothing goes out in her name without her consent. It also means respecting her relationship with her listeners, which she built and which isn’t ours to mine.

In practice, any research we do stays within public information or environments we’ve been explicitly authorized to test. We won’t surveil audiences, build covert profiles of listeners, or fabricate engagement. And we’ll be careful with claims: if we can’t show that something is secure, we won’t say it is.
