The last time I wrote about Cristiana Love, I was covering [“SONORA.”](https://projectkhaled.com/cristiana-love-sonora/) This time, I want to talk about something her new lyric video got me thinking about as a writer. I’ve been writing and drafting a drawing book ahead of our graphic novel. That might sound like taking the long way around. But put a sketchbook next to a song in development and the order starts to make sense. Before I can ask what happens to a character on page thirty, I need to know how to draw her walking into a room on page one.

That’s why I put Cris’s video at the top. Give it one play before you read any further. Pick a line you like and pay attention to which word carries it. Then try reading that line to yourself. Did you put the emphasis in the same place she did?

## Why the drawing book comes first

A graphic novel asks a lot of a single drawing. A character has to look like herself from across the room, in profile, in bad light, and halfway through turning around because somebody just said her name. A portrait can look great on its own. The harder question is whether I can draw that same person five panels later and whether you, the reader, can tell what changed in her mood.

The drawing book gives me room to work that out. Say I want to draw a character who hears something funny and is determined to keep a straight face. I could write “she’s trying not to laugh” under the panel. But can I get you to see it in the corner of her mouth? What if the better drawing is the next one, where she turns her head because she knows she’s about to crack? Those are the kinds of decisions I want to spend time on before a whole scene depends on them.

Then there’s the writing. When you draft a drawing book, you have to explain a choice clearly enough that somebody else can try it. “Make her look amused” doesn’t give the reader much to do with a pencil. “Keep the eyes steady and lift one corner of the mouth” gives them something they can draw, change, and judge for themselves. Writing that instruction also makes me a more deliberate storyteller. By the time I get to the graphic novel, I have a better idea of what the drawing can say on its own and where dialogue earns its space.

## A lyric has to work in somebody’s voice

A songwriter faces a version of that same problem. A line can read beautifully in a notebook and feel awkward the second you try to sing it. Maybe there are too many syllables before the beat lands. Maybe the word carrying the most emotion is the hardest one to hold. You can change the lyric, stretch the melody, leave some silence, or let the vocal come in a fraction later. Each choice changes what the listener hears in the very same words.

Here’s a little example of my own. Say “I knew you’d come back” out loud. Lean on *knew* and it sounds confident, maybe even a little smug. Lean on *you* and suddenly it matters who came through the door. Leave a breath before *back* and there’s room for doubt. Five words, three possible scenes. A songwriter can make that decision with a voice. In a graphic novel, I might make it by choosing whose face you see when the door opens.

That’s what I enjoy about the lyric-video format. You get the words and the performance at the same time. Your eyes can move ahead and finish a line while the singer is still holding the middle of it. For that split second, you’ve got your reading of the line and the artist’s reading playing side by side. On a second listen, try catching the breath before a phrase you like. Sometimes that little bit of space is what makes you remember the words that follow.

## What changes when the camera comes in

Now imagine developing that five-word line into a full music video. Where would you put the camera? If you show the person walking through the door first, the audience gets the surprise along with the singer. If you stay on the singer’s face, we have to work out who arrived from the reaction. Cut before the beat and the entrance can feel abrupt. Hold the shot through the silence and the room starts to feel like somewhere somebody has been waiting for a long time.

That’s where the parallel to our graphic novel really clicks for me. In comics, I get to decide what you see before you turn the page. I can end a right-hand page with a character looking up, then let you discover who she sees when you turn it. In a music video, the director can hold that same reveal until a vocal comes back in. Both choices depend on groundwork the audience may never see: the expression somebody drew six ways, the line somebody sang at three different speeds, the pause that finally felt right.

And the drawing book deserves to be enjoyed in its own right. Somebody can open it, sit down with a pencil, and make a face on the page that’s a little different from mine. I like that about a lyric video, too. Somebody can learn the words, sing them in their own voice, and come away with a favorite line. A finished graphic novel or a full music video offers another experience. There’s something worth spending time with at this scale.

Cris inspired two books I’ve put out through ProjectKhaled. One reason I wanted to share her music alongside them is that writing about inspiration can get vague fast. Here’s the most concrete way I can put it: she can hold a word for one more beat, and I can give a character one more silent panel. In both cases, that extra moment gives the audience time to feel something before the story moves on.

So give the video another play. If a line sticks with you, try imagining it as a single panel. Who’s in the frame? What do you see on their face? And if you could draw only the moment before the line or the moment after it, which one would you pick?
